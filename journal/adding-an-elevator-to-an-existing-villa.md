Most villa elevators are planned with the house. Many are not. A family wants to stay in a home they love as stairs get harder, or a renovation finally makes room for the lift that was never built.

An existing house sets limits a new build doesn't. You may not be able to dig a pit. The ceiling on the top floor may be low. The only free space may put the doors on two different sides. Each of these points to a different structure.

## Start from what you cannot change

Look at the house and name the one thing you can't move.

- No room for a pit. The Strong-Drive structure needs no pit, has a low headroom requirement and a lightweight frame. It is the layout we suggest most often for an existing home.
- A very small space. The Small Gantry fits extra-small shafts with minimal change to the building. If that small space also has no pit, compare it with Strong-Drive.
- Doors on adjacent or three sides. The Backpack Frame puts the frame behind the cabin, which frees the sides for right-angle and three-way door layouts. It also has a low construction threshold and low headroom.
- Heavy daily use. If the house can take it, the Traction Gantry gives the smoothest ride, high load capacity and easy maintenance.

The [structure guide](journal-choosing-a-shaft-and-drive-structure.html) compares all four side by side.

## A shaft that sits inside the house

In an existing home the shaft often stands in a stairwell or a double-height hall rather than inside the walls. That makes its frame part of the interior.

A steel frame is the cost-effective choice. Your own contractor clads it afterwards to match the room. An aluminium alloy frame with laminated safety glass stands on its own, takes no extra footprint and lets light through, which suits a hall you don't want to close in.

## What installation looks like

The work follows the same four stages in any house:

1. Pit and shaft preparation. Pit depth, overhead height and floor openings are checked against the drawing before anything is fitted.
2. Structure and guide rails. These are fixed to the building, not freestanding, because they carry the cabin's full load.
3. Cabin and running gear. The cabin is aligned on the rails, then the walls, ceiling, floor and doors go in.
4. Commissioning. Call panels, safety systems and door interlocks are tested floor by floor before handover.

When it is finished, each floor sees only a landing door set flush into the wall, with the call button beside it.
